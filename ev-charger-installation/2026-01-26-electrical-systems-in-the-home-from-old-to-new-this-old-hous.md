Understanding Basic Electrical Terms and Components

Grasping fundamental electrical concepts helps homeowners communicate effectively with professionals and make informed decisions about their electrical systems. Current, Voltage, and Wattage

Electricity in homes is measured in several ways. Current, measured in amperes (amps), represents the flow of electricity. Voltage, measured in volts, is the electrical pressure driving the current. Wattage, the product of amps and volts, measures the electrical power used. Most U. S. homes receive 120/240-volt alternating current (AC) power, with specific appliances requiring 240 volts.

This carrying of electricity, called current, is measured in amps.”

Your service panel will contain a series of switches that control different electrical loads used throughout the house. An average-sized house is likely to have at least several lighting circuits, several receptacle (aka outlet) circuits, plus circuits that control major appliances like the furnace, clothes dryer, water heater, etc.

All circuit breakers can be manually switched at the service panel if you need to deactivate an electrical circuit that’s being worked on. But these devices are also designed to switch off (trip) automatically when a potential safety hazard is detected. Standard circuit breakers will trip in response to excessive current draw that can damage wiring and cause a fire hazard by overheating.

A circuit breaker designated as GFCI (Ground Fault Circuit Interrupter) will also trip automatically when current leakage is detected (a safety hazard that can occur when electric wires get wet). A breaker designated as an Arc Fault Circuit Interrupter (AFCI) will trip in response to overloading and sparks.

NOTE: Beginning in the 1960s, fuse boxes were phased out in favor of electrical systems controlled by circuit breakers. It’s important to replace an old fuse box with a circuit breaker system as soon as possible —not just for code compliance, but also for safety and convenience. Residential Electric Code Requirements

Electrical code requirements stipulate where different types of breakers are used. For example, the receptacle circuits in bathrooms, kitchens, garages, basements, and other wet (or potentially wet) areas need GFCI protection. Building codes in many areas now require AFCI breakers for other household circuits, because their spark detection circuitry can protect against electrical fires.

Circuit breakers that feed receptacles will be rated at 15 or 20amps; this means they will automatically trip if current exceeds these ratings. Lighting circuits are controlled by 15amp breakers. Your service panel will also contain a limited number of larger “double-pole” breakers that have higher amp ratings for big appliances like stoves and clothes dryers. Lighting

What Are Some Common Electrical Problems in Older Homes?

Older homes often face electrical challenges due to outdated systems and components. Recognizing these issues is the first step toward creating a safer living environment. Outdated Wiring and Insufficient Power

Is 50-year-old wiring safe? While age alone doesn’t necessarily make wiring unsafe, older systems often can’t handle modern power demands. Homes built before the 1970s may have aluminum wiring, which can pose fire risks due to its tendency to expand and contract. Additionally, older homes typically have fewer outlets and lower amperage service, leading to overloaded circuits and frequent tripping. Ungrounded Circuits and Fuse Boxes

Many older homes lack proper grounding, evident in two-prong outlets. This absence of grounding increases shock risks and can damage sensitive electronics. Fuse boxes, while functional, offer less protection and convenience than modern circuit breakers. Upgrading to a grounded system with circuit breakers significantly enhances safety and reliability. Safety Hazards and Code Violations

Outdated electrical systems often violate current safety codes. Common issues include lack of GFCI protection in wet areas, overloaded circuits, and improper wire splices. These violations not only pose safety risks but can also complicate insurance coverage and home sales. Regular inspections by licensed electricians can identify and address these hazards.

Pro Tip: As This Old House electrician Scott explains: “An overload is I plug too many devices into an outlet. I’ve got the hair dryer, the space heater and the toaster going. It pulls too much energy across that wire—that heats up and can create a fire on the wire.” A short circuit, on the other hand, happens when a wire is accidentally cut, and the breaker will simply trip off. “You’ll notice it when you go down to the box and you can turn it back on and reset. It can go all the way off or sometimes it’ll go in the middle,” he adds.

What Safety Features Can Be Found in Modern Electrical Systems?

Today’s electrical systems incorporate advanced safety features to protect against various hazards

and shocks. These technologies significantly reduce the risk of electrical fires and ensure a safer home environment. GFCI and AFCI Protection

Ground Fault Circuit Interrupters (GFCIs) protect against ground faults, cutting power when they detect current leakage. They’re required in areas prone to moisture, such as kitchens, bathrooms, and outdoor outlets. Arc Fault Circuit Interrupters (AFCIs) detect electrical arcs that can cause fires, making them crucial for bedroom circuits and other living areas where fires can start from damaged or overheated wiring. Smoke and Carbon Monoxide Detectors

Modern electrical systems often include hardwired smoke and carbon monoxide detectors with battery backups. These interconnected systems provide early warning of fire or gas leaks by sounding an alarm throughout the home when danger is detected. Regular testing and maintenance of these devices are essential for optimal protection, ensuring batteries are replaced and devices are functional. Whole-House Surge Protection

Surge protectors installed at the main service panel defend against voltage spikes from lightning strikes or utility issues. These devices protect all the electronics and appliances in your home, complementing point-of-use surge protectors for sensitive electronics. This comprehensive surge protection can significantly extend the lifespan of your devices and reduce the risk of damage due to power surges. Backup Power Solutions

Power outages can disrupt daily life and pose safety risks. Modern electrical systems can incorporate various backup power options to maintain essential services during outages. Portable Generators

Portable generators offer a flexible solution for temporary power needs. They can run essential appliances and lights during short outages. However, they require manual setup and fuel management. It’s crucial to operate portable generators in well-ventilated areas to prevent carbon monoxide poisoning and to store fuel safely. Standby Generators

Permanently installed standby generators automatically activate during power failures, providing seamless backup power to the entire home or selected circuits. While more expensive than portable options, standby generators offer greater convenience and capacity. They run on natural gas or propane, automatically self-test to ensure reliability, and can power more of the home’s systems for longer periods. Battery Backup Systems

Battery systems, often paired with solar installations, store energy for use during outages or peak demand periods. These silent, emission-free options are gaining popularity as technology improves and costs decrease. Batteries can be integrated into a home’s power system to provide electricity for essential circuits for hours or even days, depending on capacity and energy usage. Photovoltaic Systems

Harnessing solar energy to generate electricity is a great way to cut your utility costs while helping to save the planet. To further sweeten the deal, government incentives for renewable energy can help homeowners offset the cost of a PV system. To learn what incentives apply in your area, visit the Database of State Incentives for Renewable Energy.

If solar orientation is favorable, PV panels can be installed on the roof of a building, or on a ground array. The electricity generated by a PV system can be used in several ways. It can feed into your main service panel to provide household electricity. If your PV system is generating more power than you can consume, this excess electricity is supplied to your electric utility. In states with net metering laws, your electric utility must pay you for this excess power.

A final option is for your PV system to store solar electricity in a battery backup system. This enables you to use solar electricity after the sun goes down. By combining a PV system with battery backup, you can maintain electrical service when there’s a power outage—an alternative to backup power supplied by a generator.
