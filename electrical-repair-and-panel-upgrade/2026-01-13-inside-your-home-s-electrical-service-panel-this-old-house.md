# Inside Your Home’s Electrical Service Panel - This Old House

Skip to content iStock

Major power outages are easy to understand: Your house loses electrical power when a storm takes down the power lines or a utility crew needs to make repairs in your area.

But what about the many smaller power outages that can occur in your house, such as when an appliance or power tool stops working or when the recessed lights in a room suddenly go out? To solve the mystery behind these mini-outages, look at your home’s main electrical service panel or breaker box—the distribution center for all the electricity you consume. Understanding how your electrical panel works can help you troubleshoot issues, perform basic maintenance, and know when to call a professional, all of which will help preserve your home’s safety and functionality.

Electrical Service Panel Anatomy: A Big Box of Circuit Breakers

Your home’s main electrical service panel was once called a fuse box because it contained all the main fuses for different electrical circuits throughout the house. Today, however, a service panel is often called a “breaker box” because it’s full of circuit breakers, which are safer and more convenient than fuses.

The main service panel is typically located in a home’s basement or utility room. Some homes also have a subpanel—a smaller version of the main panel—to control electrical circuits in a garage, workshop, or other outbuilding. A service panel is easy to spot. It’s a gray steel box with a hinged door, securely attached to the wall at around shoulder height. Inside the box, you’ll find two rows of switches. These are the circuit breakers you can toggle on and off to control the electricity you use in different parts of your house.

Pro Tip: Heath Eastman, Ask This Old House electrician, advises that before removing a panel cover, you should always locate and shut off the main breaker first: “I want to go outside and turn the main breaker off, lock this out so I can take this cover off safely.” In some homes, the main breaker is remotely mounted—outside or in a separate enclosure—rather than inside the panel itself, so be sure you know where yours is before starting any work.

SAFETY ALERT: Never remove this cover plate unless you flip the main switch to turn off all the power.

Above the circuit breakers, you’ll find a larger main switch—sometimes known as the service disconnect—that controls all the power available to the house from your electric utility. Behind the cover plate surrounding all the panel’s switches, you’ll find three large wires entering the box from the main power line and many smaller wires that connect individual circuit breakers to electrical cables that run to different circuits throughout your house. Main Components of an Electrical Panel

The main components of an electrical panel include the following:

Bus bars: Metal bars that distribute electricity to circuit breakers

Circuit breakers: Smaller switches that control individual circuits

Not all breakers are interchangeable. As the Ask This Old House team points out during home inspections, “You can’t just interchange whichever breaker you want with whichever panel it happens to fit in.” Check the label on your panel—it will tell you the manufacturer and exactly which breakers are acceptable to install. Using the wrong breaker is a serious safety hazard.

You can use the main breaker or service disconnect switch at the top of the panel to cut off all power to your home when necessary. The Role of Circuit Breakers

Circuit breakers are the workhorses of your electrical panel. These switches serve two primary functions as follows:

Automatic protection: Breakers trip to cut power and prevent overheating and potential fire hazards when they detect overloads or faults, such as when a circuit draws more current than it’s designed to handle.

Manual control: You can turn off power to specific circuits for maintenance or repairs. Know Your Circuit Breakers

You’ll find a number next to each circuit breaker (stamped into the steel cover plate) that enables you to match individual breakers to specific circuits. Once you’ve identified what each circuit controls, you can fill in the lined sheet of paper glued to the back of the panel door with clear labels. Typical circuit breaker labels might include: “kitchen outlets,” “living room lights,” or “clothes dryer,” for example. An average-sized house will likely have at least several lighting circuits, receptacle (outlet) circuits, and circuits that control major appliances like the furnace, clothes dryer, or water heater.

A circuit breaker is an electronic switch that lets you manually deactivate a circuit at the service panel. You’d need to do this if you were doing electrical wiring work that might put you in contact with live wires, such as replacing a ceiling fan or installing a dimmer switch. Circuit breakers receive classifications based on their type and amperage rating, which specifies how much current the breaker can handle before shutting off.

Regardless of classification, all circuit breakers are designed to turn off automatically when the electricity flowing through the circuit exceeds the breaker’s power or amperage rating. This can happen when too many devices are running on one circuit, like if you use a heavy-duty vacuum in the garage while someone else uses a circular saw. By shutting down in response to high amperage draw, the breaker protects against overloads, short circuits, and overheated wiring that can cause an electrical fire and damage electrical equipment. Standard Circuit Breakers

Standard circuit breakers are the most common breaker type found in residential panels. These breakers have a typical amperage rating of 15 or 20 amps for general household circuits, with larger ratings for major appliances. GFCI Circuit Breakers

Ground Fault Circuit Interrupter (GFCI) breakers provide extra protection against electric shock. They monitor the balance of current between the hot and neutral wires and trip if they detect even a small imbalance. Such imbalances could indicate a ground fault, which is when electricity follows an unintentional path to a grounded surface. GFCI protection is required in areas where water may be present, such as the following: Bathrooms Garages Kitchens Outdoor outlets AFCI Circuit Breakers

Arc Fault Circuit Interrupter (AFCI) breakers detect electrical arcing, which is when a current jumps a space within a circuit or between conductors. Arcing can occur due to damaged wires or loose connections and can cause buzzing outlets, flickering lights, and tripped circuit breakers. These breakers help prevent electrical fires by shutting off power when they sense dangerous arcing conditions. AFCI protection is now required in many areas of new and renovated homes, including the following: Bedrooms Dining rooms Kitchens Living rooms Additional Safety Features

Circuit breakers do more than protect a home and its inhabitants from electrical hazards. They can also provide valuable diagnostic information. If a standard circuit breaker trips, one or more devices might be drawing too much power from the circuit. If a GFCI breaker controlling kitchen outlets keeps tripping, it indicates a current leakage somewhere in the circuit that you’ll need to locate and repair. In rare cases, this can also indicate a faulty circuit breaker. When an AFCI breaker trips, it can be due to arcing caused by loose or improper wiring.

Pro Tip: Arc-fault circuit interrupter (AFCI) protection is now required in all new construction, and GFCI protection is required in bathrooms, basements, outdoors, and kitchens. If you still have older standard breakers in your panel, Scott Caron recommends upgrading: “This is a good time to update them to either arc fault or GFCI.” Decoding Your Electrical Panel’s Layout

Understanding how to read your electrical panel can help you quickly identify and address issues when they arise. Reading Circuit Labels

Each circuit breaker in your panel should be labeled to indicate which area or appliance it controls. If your panel isn’t labeled or the labels are outdated, consider creating a new, accurate labeling system. This can save time and frustration when you need to shut off power to a specific area. Identifying Amperage Ratings

Circuit breakers are rated based on the amount of current they can safely handle. You’ll find these ratings stamped on the switch of each breaker. Common residential amperage ratings include the following:

15 amps: Lighting circuits and general-use outlets

20 amps: Kitchen and laundry room outlets

30 amps: Large appliances like electric dryers

50 amps: Electric ranges or large air conditioners

Knowing these ratings can help you understand which circuits can handle higher-powered devices and when you might need to upgrade a circuit for increased capacity.

Pro Tip: Heath, master electrician on Ask This Old House, points out that panels can be deceiving: “A lot of times with these panels, even though we have a certain number of spaces, it may take more circuits than it actually shows.” If your panel looks full, a professional can assess whether tandem breakers or a subpanel might give you the additional capacity you need.

Common Electrical Panel Issues and Troubleshooting

Even well-maintained electrical panels can experience issues from time to time. Recognizing common problems can help you determine when to attempt a simple fix and when to call in a professional. Frequent Circuit Tripping

If a circuit breaker trips repeatedly, it’s often a sign that the circuit is overloaded. This can happen if you use too many high-powered devices on the same circuit. Follow these steps to troubleshoot:

Unplug some devices from the affected circuit.

Reset the breaker by switching it fully off and then back on.

If the problem persists, consider redistributing devices to other circuits or upgrading the circuit’s capacity.

If a GFCI breaker keeps tripping, it may indicate a ground fault somewhere in the circuit. This could be due to damaged wiring or a faulty appliance and you should hire a licensed electrician to investigate. Signs of an Outdated Panel

Older electrical panels may not be equipped to handle a modern home’s power demands. Signs that your panel may be outdated include the following:

Burning smells or buzzing sounds from the panel Flickering or dimming lights Frequent tripping or blown fuses Fuses instead of circuit breakers Warm or discolored breakers or wires

If you notice any of these signs, hire a professional to inspect your panel as soon as possible. An outdated panel can pose serious safety risks and you may need to upgrade it.

Safety Precautions When Working With Your Electrical Panel

Safety should always be your top priority when dealing with electricity. Even simple tasks involving your electrical panel can be dangerous if you don’t take certain precautions. Essential Safety Gear

Before working on or around your electrical panel, ensure you have the following safety equipment: Flashlight Insulated gloves Rubber-soled shoes Safety glasses Voltage tester

Never work on your electrical panel if the floor around it is wet or if you’re standing in water. Steps To Safely Turn Off Power

If you need to turn off power to your entire home, follow these steps:

Inform all household members of the impending power shutoff.

Locate the main breaker at the top of the panel.

Firmly grasp the handle and quickly switch it to the “Off” position.

Use a voltage tester to confirm that the power is off before proceeding with any work.

Remember, even with the main breaker off, the large wires connecting the utility supply to your panel are still energized. Never attempt to work on these wires yourself. Upgrading Your Electrical Service Panel

The Lifespan of Residential Electrical Panels

Like any home component, electrical panels have a finite lifespan. Familiarizing yourself with the factors that affect your panel’s longevity can help you plan for its eventual replacement. Factors Affecting Panel Longevity

Several factors can impact how long your electrical panel will last, such as the following:

Environmental conditions (humidity, temperature extremes) Frequency of use and electrical demand Quality of initial installation Regular maintenance and inspections

Technological advancements in electrical safety

On average, a well-maintained electrical panel can last 25–40 years. However, this can vary significantly based on the factors mentioned above. Our Conclusion
