# How to Design an Entry Garden - This Old House

Skip to content Making an Entrance Photo by John Gruen

Consider it a manifesto against foundation plantings, those familiar beds of rhododendron, yew, holly, and euonymus first used to cloak the high foundation walls of tall Victorian-era homes. “Foundation planting has become a safe cliche—it’s time to move on,” proclaims Gordon Hayward, who works nationwide as a garden designer and lecturer, and is the author of 10 books on landscape design. Hayward is a proponent of the entry garden, wide borders flanking a front walk that serves as the garden’s spine. “I want you to walk through and experience the front garden rather than pass it by,” he says.

Landscape designer Julie Moir Messervy, author of Home Outside, echoes this philosophy: “Your home doesn’t start at the front door. A well-designed entry should have layers of elements that extend a welcome from the curb to the stoop.”

A fine example is the entry garden he designed for the 1860 house shown here. To give the historic house’s facade its due, homeowner Ramsey Yoder and his late wife, Patty, first commissioned stone steps and low walls to terrace the hillside and emphasize the front-door focal point. Then they called on Hayward to design a carefully composed garden that would deliver summerlong color, winter interest, and a mix of textures.

He started, as always, by creating the winter garden first, placing evergreen and deciduous shrubs for year-round structure. Then he planned a sequence of blooming perennials and filled in with annuals, placing any fragrant plants close to the path. The resulting garden ties the house into the surrounding landscape, and, just as important, says Hayward, “it creates a sense of welcome, for your friends, your family, and yourself, too.”

When laying out your entry garden, start by creating planting beds on either side of the front walk that are at least as wide as the path itself. A bell-shaped border that widens toward the corners of the house creates a visual frame that draws the eye toward the front door.

Pro Tip: “In mixed borders, aim to juxtapose textures that contrast with one another. Generally, leaves are categorized as broad, strap shaped, or frilly. So you might place a broad-leaved hosta next to a frilly-leaved fern next to a strappy daylily for maximum interest.”—Gordon Hayward, garden designer Four Seasons of Interest Illustration by Rodica Prato

A strong framework of evergreen trees and shrubs provides an unchanging backdrop in this Vermont garden, while flowering perennials and annuals provide lots of color all summer and into the fall.

With careful planning, there can be something in a garden to delight the senses at every time of year. Spring-flowering daffodils, tulips, forsythia, azaleas, lilacs, and dogwoods give way to summer-flowering poppies, peonies, daylilies, iris, catmint, astilbe, and hydrangeas. Annual petunias and impatiens maintain their color until fall, when Japanese maples go fiery red. Shade-loving hostas and golden hakone grass brighten beds with their foliage alone.
