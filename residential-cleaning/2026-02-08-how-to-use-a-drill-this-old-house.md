Types of Drill Drivers

When choosing a drill driver, think about the type that best suits your needs. These are the three main categories:

Light-duty: These are ideal for small projects and tasks around the house, like assembling furniture and driving small screws.

Medium-duty: These drivers offer more power and versatility than their light-duty counterparts. They’re best suited for working with most wood species and driving medium-sized holes.

Heavy-duty: These drill drivers are built for demanding tasks and professional use—think driving large screws and lag bolts or working with masonry. Drill Driver Configurations

When shopping for a drill driver, you’ll encounter two main configurations: bare tools and kits. Bare tools are sold without batteries or chargers, making them more affordable but requiring compatible batteries. Kits include the drill driver, batteries, and a charger, providing everything needed to get started, but are more expensive. Key Features of Modern Drill Drivers

Larger chuck sizes generally indicate more powerful drills capable of handling bigger bits. Clutch

The adjustable clutch allows you to preset the torque applied by the drill driver. This feature helps prevent the splitting wood, stripping screws, and overdriving fasteners. A higher number of clutch settings provides more precise control over the tool’s torque output. Drive Type Selector

Many modern drill drivers offer multiple drive types, including the following:

Drilling mode: This is the standard mode used for creating holes in various materials like wood, plastic, or metal. In drilling mode, the drill driver operates at high speeds with lower torque, making it ideal for quickly drilling precise holes without damaging the material.

Driving mode: Designed for driving screws and fasteners into surfaces, driving mode operates at lower speeds with higher torque. This provides more control to prevent over-tightening or stripping screws.

Hammer drilling mode (on some models): Available on select drill drivers, hammer drilling mode adds a pulsing hammer action to the rotation, making it easier to drill into tough surfaces like concrete, brick, or masonry. The rapid hammering motion helps break up the hard material while the drill bit continues to rotate.

This versatility allows you to switch between different tasks without changing tools. Speed Selector

The speed selector typically offers low speed with high torque and high speed with lower torque. This feature allows you to optimize the drill’s performance for different applications. LED Work Light

An integrated LED work light illuminates the work area, improving visibility and accuracy. This feature is especially useful when working in dimly lit spaces. Motor Type

Drill drivers are available with the following two types of motors:

Brushed motors: More affordable but less efficient

Brushless motors: More efficient, powerful, and longer-lasting, but typically more expensive

The battery is a crucial component of cordless drill drivers. With advancements in battery technology, modern drill drivers offer long-lasting power and faster charging times. When evaluating batteries, consider these factors:

Voltage: Higher voltage generally means more power, but it can also result in a heavier tool. Common options include 12-volt, 18-volt, and 20-volt batteries.

Ampere-hours (Ah): This rating measures the battery’s capacity. Higher Ah ratings provide longer run times.

To get the most out of your drill driver while staying safe, follow these best practices:

Wear protective gear: Always wear safety glasses to protect your eyes from debris. Consider using ear protection if you’re working with a particularly loud drill.

Use appropriate bits: Make sure you’re using the correct bit for the material and task at hand.

Secure the material: Clamp down wood or other materials to keep them from moving while you work, which improves accuracy and safety.

Start slowly: Begin with a lower speed to create a pilot hole if needed, then gradually increase speed.

Maintain your tool: Keep your drill driver clean, check for any signs of wear, and store it in a dry place. Regular maintenance can prolong the tool’s lifespan and maintain its performance.

Pro Tip: Tom Silva, This Old House contractor, cautions: “You might be holding a board for a quick hole and end up drilling through your hand on the other side.” Always pay attention to where your hands are at all times, and never hold a workpiece in your palm directly behind where the bit will exit.
