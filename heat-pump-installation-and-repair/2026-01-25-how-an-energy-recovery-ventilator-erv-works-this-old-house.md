Energy recovery ventilators (ERVs) bring fresh air into your home while maintaining indoor comfort and lowering energy costs. These systems prove especially useful in tightly sealed, well-insulated houses where stale air and pollutants can build up. This Old House plumbing and heating expert Richard Trethewey demonstrates how an ERV works in the video above. Keep reading to learn how ERVs function and why they matter for your home. What Are Energy Recovery Ventilators?

Energy recovery ventilators exchange stale indoor air with fresh outdoor air, simultaneously transferring heat and moisture between the two air streams. They’re a good choice in homes that are stuffy or prone to moisture and mold. Trethewey sums up the need for ERVs simply: “If you’re going to insulate, you’ve got to ventilate.” As building codes have demanded tighter construction for energy efficiency since 2012, requirements for whole-house mechanical ventilation have made ERVs an essential part of modern home design. The biggest benefit ERVs offer homeowners is improved ventilation, but they can also provide the following:

Improved indoor air quality: Stale air can collect contaminates, but the ERV filters out particles and allergens.

Reduced energy load: Modern ERVs can recover up to 80% of the energy from exhaust air, depending on the model and operating conditions. This means they can reduce energy costs, especially in hot and humid climates.

Consistent indoor temperature: In the winter, the ERV transfers heat from the warm exhaust air to the cold incoming air, reducing the load on your heating system. In the summer, heat from the warm incoming air is transferred to the cooler exhaust air.

Better humidity levels: ERVs transfer moisture between air streams, lowering humidity levels and reducing mold growth potential. In winter, this transfer can also help prevent dryness.

Reduced HVAC load: Since the ERV helps to maintain a consistent indoor temperature, the HVAC doesn’t have to work as hard, extending its lifespan while also lowering energy bills.

Pro tip: Ensure that your HVAC technician verifies that your existing bath vent fans send moist air outside your home rather than through your new ERV, advises Trethewey. This prevents the recirculation of moisture that can lead to mold or mildew. Cost of Installation

The cost of installing an ERV system can vary, depending on factors such as the size of your home or existing infrastructure. You can expect to pay at least $2,000 for a professionally installed ERV. The investment pays off through improved air quality and energy savings over time. Maintaining Your ERV

Your ERV needs to be regularly maintained to keep it running efficiently. Homeowners should perform the following tasks: Check and clean fan blades and housing.

Check that ductwork is free from obstructions.

Clean or replace filters every three to six months.

Inspect and clean the heat exchanger core annually.

Schedule professional servicing once a year.

Verify proper operation of controls and dampers.

Common issues with ERVs include reduced airflow due to clogged filters, unusual noises from the fan components, ineffective humidity control, and faulty controls or sensors. Some of these issues, such as clogged filters, can be repaired by homeowners. Others may require a professional. Address any problems you notice right away to prevent further damage to your ERV ERVs vs. HRVs

ERVs and heat recovery ventilators (HRVs) transfer heat between incoming and outgoing air streams. However, ERVs also transfer moisture and can help cool the home, while HRVs can’t.

ERVs are a good choice for climates with high humidity or for homes where maintaining consistent indoor humidity is a priority. HRVs can be a better choice for climates with mild humidity or in colder environments, as ERVs can have trouble functioning in cold temperatures.
